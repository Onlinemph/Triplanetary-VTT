# The computer opponent

The ground game's computer player lives in `src/ogre/ai/`. It plays either
seat of every scenario in the picker — the cybertank driving on the command
post, the defence kiting it with missile tanks, an assault force grinding
toward a base — and every special order the scenarios add: deployment,
reserves, orbital strikes, cruise missiles, overruns.

It is not a search. It is a set of instincts, each a weighted sum of
features, each asked of the engine's own previews so it never proposes what
the rules refuse. The weights are the whole personality, and they are
learned: `scripts/tune-ai.ts` plays the game against itself by the
thousand and keeps what wins.

## The shape of it

| File          | What it holds                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `player.ts`   | The instincts. `aiPlan(state, map, player, weights)` returns the orders for the decision the state is waiting on, ending with the one that moves the game on.                |
| `weights.ts`  | Every weight, its hand-set default, the range the tuner may search, and a line on what it does. `DEFAULT_WEIGHTS` is the hand-set table with the tuner's findings laid over. |
| `tuned.ts`    | The learned table. Generated by the tuner; committed.                                                                                                                        |
| `simulate.ts` | Headless games for the tuner and the tests: `playGame` and `scoreFor`.                                                                                                       |

## What a counter weighs

Every decision is scored the same way: add up the features, each times its
weight, and take the best. The features are the vocabulary; the weights are
the taste.

**Where to end a move.** For each hex a unit can reach this phase:

- _goal_ — hexes of ground (not crow's flight: craters and water are not
  ground) to the objective, or to the nearest enemy cybertank when
  defending, or to the exit edge when leaving is the goal;
- _exit goals_ — a scenario that is about leaving says so in its data
  (`exitEdge`, `exitSide`, and `exitUnits` when only some counters have to
  make it). The counters that have to go head for the edge and walk off it;
  the rest of their side stays with them; the other side goes for them. A
  breakthrough in the custom battle sends the whole attacking force; The
  Train sends the train (which also opens its speed up a step a turn, since
  the speed is set before it moves) with the escort alongside and the
  raiders after it. A "D" is worth nothing against the train, and the fire
  planner knows it;
- _standoff_ — how far inside its own reach the nearest enemy would be,
  for a gun that would rather hold at its range than walk in;
- _in reach_ — what the enemies inside its range are worth;
- _kill_ — what its guns could expect to destroy from there this turn,
  worked out on the Combat Results Table for every target in range,
  cybertanks taken at their most valuable gun or their treads;
- _threat_ — how much enemy fire can reach the hex next phase, over the
  unit's own defence;
- _cover_, _hazards_, _water_ for a cybertank, _crowding_ a friend;
- _screen_ — how far off the line between the enemy cybertank and what we
  guard; _block_ — whether the hex lies on that cybertank's shortest road.

Each of these has a weight per **role** — cybertank, armour, GEV, infantry,
artillery, support — because combined arms is the observation that a
howitzer, a GEV and a Mark V want different things from the same hex.

**What to shoot.** Targets are valued by victory points, attack strength,
and how much of their fire currently reaches our units (_threat relief_).
A cybertank's guns are valued by kind; its treads by how many a hit would
strip and how few are left. The strongest guns in range are stacked on a
target until another gun stops improving the expected value; a one-shot
missile carries its own cost, so it is kept for something worth it.

**Ramming and overrunning.** The command post always; armour when the
treads can spare it; and whatever corks the only lane through the craters,
because there is no way round it.

**The second movement.** A GEV that has fired gets away. Each hex it can
reach is scored by the _expected loss_ of standing there — the odds the
enemy fire that can reach it would get against the GEV, read off the CRT,
times what the GEV is worth — and by what its own guns could kill from
there next turn. A cybertank's one-shot missiles count in that fire at a
weight the tuner sets, because a cybertank that keeps its missiles for a
howitzer or the post is not much of a threat to a GEV at range five.

**Deployment, reserves, strikes, cruise missiles.** A counter looks for the
setup hex it would rather start in; a reserve for the entry hex nearest the
objective and farthest from the guns; a strike for the target it is likeliest
to kill (never the base — the fleet supports the drop, it does not do its
job for it); a cruise missile for the hex where the blast hurts them most
and us least.

## How it learns

`npx tsx scripts/tune-ai.ts --generations 16 --population 28 --seeds 2`

A cross-entropy search. Each generation samples a population of weight
tables around the current mean, plays every one of them through every
scenario, from every seat, against the reigning table, with the same dice
seeds for everyone. The best quarter become the new mean; the spread shrinks
where they agree. A yardstick game against the hand-set baseline is played
each generation so progress is measured against something that does not
move. Games run in parallel across the cores.

The only teacher is the score: a win counts +1 (complete), +0.85
(standard) or +0.7 (marginal), a loss the same below zero, plus the points
margin at up to ±0.5, so a table that keeps its counters alive is told
apart from one that reaches the same verdict without them. Nothing tells
the tuner to missile a howitzer at range five; it finds that the tables
that do so win.

The run writes `src/ogre/ai/tuned.ts` and `docs/ai-tuning-report.md`, and
checkpoints in `.tune/` so `--resume` picks a run back up.

## What it learned

See `docs/ai-tuning-report.md` for the runs (four of them, 43,442 games),
the evaluation and the weights that moved most. In play:

- **Treads are the target.** A tread hit is worth three and a half times
  what the hand-set table gave it, and the defence's armour and artillery
  close on the cybertank to shoot them.
- **Missiles are kept.** A one-shot missile carries a cost of about ten
  points, so it goes to a howitzer, the post, or something it will kill;
  with two aboard, though, the learned Ogre will still spend one on a GEV
  at range five, and the second-move scoring knows it — nothing within
  five hexes of a cybertank with missiles is safe.
- **Rams are for the post**, and for a tank corking the only lane through
  the craters; the threshold for anything else rose from eight to
  twenty-four.
- **Garrison cybertanks and artillery screen the base**, standing on the
  line between the enemy cybertank and what they guard.
- **Hit and run.** A GEV that has fired at close range on a cybertank whose
  missiles are spent ends its second move outside the main battery's three,
  so that killing it costs the cybertank its own move. That took a change
  of feature, not of weight: the second move is scored by the odds of
  being killed where it ends, read off the CRT, and by what its own guns
  could do from there, and not by the worth of what stands in reach — a
  sixty-point cybertank two hexes away is not a reason to stay.

The tactics the tests pin down (`tests/ogre/ai-tactics.test.ts`): a
cybertank closes on a howitzer to exactly missile range and kills it with
a missile; a missile tank against a cybertank whose missiles are spent
fires from its own range, three or four, outside the secondaries; the
GEV above; and a cybertank walks out of a crater pocket.

## The war: the general staff

Orbital Drop is a war above the ground game, and the pilot in `src/ai/`
does not know what a war is for. `src/ai/war/` is the general staff that
does: it reads the war off the state and hands the pilot errands, and the
pilot flies them.

| File          | What it holds                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `staff.ts`    | The decisions: what to buy, where to fly, when to declare, when to land. Each is asked at its phase from `nextCommand` and answers with an ordinary command.                     |
| `weights.ts`  | Every weight, its hand-set default, the range the tuner may search, and a line on what it does. `DEFAULT_WAR_WEIGHTS` is the hand-set table with the tuner's findings laid over. |
| `tuned.ts`    | The learned table. Generated by the tuner; committed.                                                                                                                            |
| `simulate.ts` | Whole wars headless — the fleet game through the pilot and the staff, every ground battle through the ground game's computer — for the tuner and the tests.                      |

**The target.** Every base the staff does not hold is scored: a planetary
base is worth more than a rock, a base nobody owns (militia and no more) a
little more than an enemy's, and against that the trip from the wave — the
loaded transports, or the forward yard before anything is loaded — the
part of the trip a transport's tanks cannot fly, live planetary guns, and,
before the wave sails, the enemy strength seen around it. A wave in flight
locks on to the world it is nearest, because a transport cannot outrun what
it finds and cannot turn round on the fuel it has left; the base an
invasion has already been declared against keeps a bonus of its own. The
rock inside the pirate cordon is never a target (§8.01).

**The landing force.** The staff prices a base's defence at its militia
plus a prior for what an enemy might have bought, wants a landing worth a
multiple of that, and fills each transport at the forward yard — the own
base nearest the target — from one shared purse, in proportion to its
appetites for infantry, heavy tanks, missile tanks, GEVs and howitzers,
with a reserve kept back. Four free holds and the appetite for it ship a
Mark III in modules (§2.02). A hold stays on the pad until it holds what it
was planned to; then it sails.

**The assault.** Warships ride with the wave and hold in orbit over the
target; the first one over the hexside silences the guns (§4.02, permanent
here). The invasion is declared (§4.01) once enough of the wave's worth is
in orbit and a ship is overhead — against live guns only with enough escort
to make the two-day window a fair bet — and the wave goes down the day
after (§4.04) on the declared hexside, never while the guns are live. If the
window shuts first, the staff declares again.

**Garrisons, hulls and the shop.** Each base wants a garrison worth a
standing sum plus more for every enemy ship and transport seen near it; the
staff buys for the base furthest below that, infantry or armour by appetite
and room under the caps (§3.02), a share of it into the reaction force
(§3.03), a cybertank when that is the first appetite and the armour slot is
empty — all inside a share of the war chest it will sink into standing
forces. Transports and warships are commissioned at the forward yard when
the fleet is short and the purse is not; a worn cybertank goes to the shop
(§7) when the purse can stand the quote.

**What it does not see.** §3.02 records a garrison's composition secretly.
With fog of war on, `src/net/redact.ts` sends each seat only the garrisons
of the bases it holds; without fog the staff simply does not look, and
`tests/ai-war.test.ts` pins that an enemy's hidden Mark V changes nothing
about its choices.

**The frozen sky.** §4.05: with the landers down, "the space game pauses".
`skyFrozen` is true while a ground battle waits, and every computer seat
says nothing — not even the phase-ending order — until it is fought. That is
what lets a headless war stop at the freeze for the battle, exactly as the
shell mounts it.

**The prize.** §6.02 captures a base intact only when the garrison is gone.
An assault says so to the ground game's computer (`scenarioData.prizeIntact`),
which then hunts the garrison down instead of shooting the base, and counts
the post or building at `fire.prize` of its usual worth only after
`fire.prizePatience` turns of that. `tests/ogre/prize.test.ts` pins that an
assault on a militia takes the base whole.

### How it learns

`npm run tune:war -- --generations 8 --population 12 --seeds 1 --days 60`

The same cross-entropy search as the ground game's, over the staff's
table: each generation samples a population of tables around the current
mean, plays every one of them through the war from both seats against the
reigning table, with the ground battles fought by the shipped ground table
on both sides so the search is over the staff alone, and moves the mean
toward the best quarter. A war is scored for a seat by its verdict when the
knife decides it, and otherwise by the net-worth margin at the day cap —
purse, hulls, holds, bases at MCr 300 and 150 (§8.04), garrisons at list —
so a table that takes and keeps a base is told apart from one that only
buys garrisons. Wars are long, so a run is small: a dozen candidates, one
seed, sixty days, a few minutes a generation across the cores. The run
writes `src/ai/war/tuned.ts` and `docs/war-tuning-report.md`, and
checkpoints in `.tune-war/` so `--resume` picks it back up.

### What it learned

See `docs/war-tuning-report.md` for the first run: eight generations, 208
wars, the reigning table ahead of the hand-set baseline by about half a
base's worth per war. A small run — a war is a thousand orders and a few
ground battles, so a generation is measured in minutes rather than
seconds — and what it moved is a first opinion rather than a settled one:

- **Bases are worth more than the hand-set table said.** A planetary base
  went from 120 to 184, a rock from 60 to 36; distance and the enemy fleet
  near the target both matter less than they did.
- **The wave waits.** Nine tenths of its worth in orbit before declaring,
  and three and a half points of escort overhead before declaring against
  live guns — a corvette is not enough; a frigate is.
- **Garrisons got heavier**, armour and cybertanks especially, and a base
  wants half again as much standing force; the reaction share stayed
  where it was.
- **The landing leans on infantry and missile tanks** rather than heavy
  tanks, with a howitzer more often than before.

In the sixty-day wars the simulator plays, either power now mounts two
invasions and takes two bases; the map's geometry keeps the two capitals
out of a transport's range of each other, so the war in that span is for
Mercury and the rocks.
