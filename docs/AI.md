# The computer opponent

The ground game's computer player lives in `src/ogre/ai/`. It plays either
seat of every scenario in the picker — the cybertank driving on the command
post, the defence kiting it with missile tanks, an assault force grinding
toward a base — and every special order the scenarios add: deployment,
reserves, orbital strikes, cruise missiles, overruns.

It is not a search. It is a set of instincts, each a weighted sum of
features, each asked of the engine's own previews so it never proposes what
the rules refuse. The weights are the whole personality, and they are
learned: `scripts/tune-ai.ts` plays the game against itself by the
thousand and keeps what wins.

## The shape of it

| File          | What it holds                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `player.ts`   | The instincts. `aiPlan(state, map, player, weights)` returns the orders for the decision the state is waiting on, ending with the one that moves the game on.                |
| `weights.ts`  | Every weight, its hand-set default, the range the tuner may search, and a line on what it does. `DEFAULT_WEIGHTS` is the hand-set table with the tuner's findings laid over. |
| `tuned.ts`    | The learned table. Generated by the tuner; committed.                                                                                                                        |
| `simulate.ts` | Headless games for the tuner and the tests: `playGame` and `scoreFor`.                                                                                                       |

## What a counter weighs

Every decision is scored the same way: add up the features, each times its
weight, and take the best. The features are the vocabulary; the weights are
the taste.

**Where to end a move.** For each hex a unit can reach this phase:

- _goal_ — hexes of ground (not crow's flight: craters and water are not
  ground) to the objective, or to the nearest enemy cybertank when
  defending, or to the exit edge when leaving is the goal;
- _standoff_ — how far inside its own reach the nearest enemy would be,
  for a gun that would rather hold at its range than walk in;
- _in reach_ — what the enemies inside its range are worth;
- _kill_ — what its guns could expect to destroy from there this turn,
  worked out on the Combat Results Table for every target in range,
  cybertanks taken at their most valuable gun or their treads;
- _threat_ — how much enemy fire can reach the hex next phase, over the
  unit's own defence;
- _cover_, _hazards_, _water_ for a cybertank, _crowding_ a friend;
- _screen_ — how far off the line between the enemy cybertank and what we
  guard; _block_ — whether the hex lies on that cybertank's shortest road.

Each of these has a weight per **role** — cybertank, armour, GEV, infantry,
artillery, support — because combined arms is the observation that a
howitzer, a GEV and a Mark V want different things from the same hex.

**What to shoot.** Targets are valued by victory points, attack strength,
and how much of their fire currently reaches our units (_threat relief_).
A cybertank's guns are valued by kind; its treads by how many a hit would
strip and how few are left. The strongest guns in range are stacked on a
target until another gun stops improving the expected value; a one-shot
missile carries its own cost, so it is kept for something worth it.

**Ramming and overrunning.** The command post always; armour when the
treads can spare it; and whatever corks the only lane through the craters,
because there is no way round it.

**The second movement.** A GEV that has fired gets away. Each hex it can
reach is scored by the _expected loss_ of standing there — the odds the
enemy fire that can reach it would get against the GEV, read off the CRT,
times what the GEV is worth — and by what its own guns could kill from
there next turn. A cybertank's one-shot missiles count in that fire at a
weight the tuner sets, because a cybertank that keeps its missiles for a
howitzer or the post is not much of a threat to a GEV at range five.

**Deployment, reserves, strikes, cruise missiles.** A counter looks for the
setup hex it would rather start in; a reserve for the entry hex nearest the
objective and farthest from the guns; a strike for the target it is likeliest
to kill (never the base — the fleet supports the drop, it does not do its
job for it); a cruise missile for the hex where the blast hurts them most
and us least.

## How it learns

`npx tsx scripts/tune-ai.ts --generations 16 --population 28 --seeds 2`

A cross-entropy search. Each generation samples a population of weight
tables around the current mean, plays every one of them through every
scenario, from every seat, against the reigning table, with the same dice
seeds for everyone. The best quarter become the new mean; the spread shrinks
where they agree. A yardstick game against the hand-set baseline is played
each generation so progress is measured against something that does not
move. Games run in parallel across the cores.

The only teacher is the score: a win counts +1 (complete), +0.85
(standard) or +0.7 (marginal), a loss the same below zero, plus the points
margin at up to ±0.5, so a table that keeps its counters alive is told
apart from one that reaches the same verdict without them. Nothing tells
the tuner to missile a howitzer at range five; it finds that the tables
that do so win.

The run writes `src/ogre/ai/tuned.ts` and `docs/ai-tuning-report.md`, and
checkpoints in `.tune/` so `--resume` picks a run back up.

## What it learned

See `docs/ai-tuning-report.md` for the runs (four of them, 43,442 games),
the evaluation and the weights that moved most. In play:

- **Treads are the target.** A tread hit is worth three and a half times
  what the hand-set table gave it, and the defence's armour and artillery
  close on the cybertank to shoot them.
- **Missiles are kept.** A one-shot missile carries a cost of about ten
  points, so it goes to a howitzer, the post, or something it will kill;
  with two aboard, though, the learned Ogre will still spend one on a GEV
  at range five, and the second-move scoring knows it — nothing within
  five hexes of a cybertank with missiles is safe.
- **Rams are for the post**, and for a tank corking the only lane through
  the craters; the threshold for anything else rose from eight to
  twenty-four.
- **Garrison cybertanks and artillery screen the base**, standing on the
  line between the enemy cybertank and what they guard.
- **Hit and run.** A GEV that has fired at close range on a cybertank whose
  missiles are spent ends its second move outside the main battery's three,
  so that killing it costs the cybertank its own move. That took a change
  of feature, not of weight: the second move is scored by the odds of
  being killed where it ends, read off the CRT, and by what its own guns
  could do from there, and not by the worth of what stands in reach — a
  sixty-point cybertank two hexes away is not a reason to stay.

The tactics the tests pin down (`tests/ogre/ai-tactics.test.ts`): a
cybertank closes on a howitzer to exactly missile range and kills it with
a missile; a missile tank against a cybertank whose missiles are spent
fires from its own range, three or four, outside the secondaries; the
GEV above; and a cybertank walks out of a crater pocket.
